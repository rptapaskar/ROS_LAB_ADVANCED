01 · DIFFERENTIAL-DRIVE KINEMATICS

Two wheels.
One instantaneous motion.

For a differential-drive robot, the two driven wheels determine the robot's forward and angular velocity. Let wheel radius be r, track width be L, and left/right wheel angular velocities be ωL and ωR.

🚀

Linear velocity

v = r/2 · (ωR + ωL)

Equal wheel motion drives forward/backward translation. The sign depends on your convention.

↻

Angular velocity

ω = r/L · (ωR − ωL)

A difference in wheel velocities creates rotation about the instantaneous centre of rotation.

🛞

Wheel linear velocity

vL = rωL   ·   vR = rωR

Angular wheel speed becomes tangential wheel speed through wheel radius.

⭕

Instantaneous centre

R = v/ω

For nonzero angular velocity, the robot follows a circular arc around an instantaneous centre of rotation.

Special cases: if ωL = ωR, ideal motion is straight. If the wheel speeds are equal and opposite, the robot rotates about its centre. Otherwise, the body follows a curved path.
02 · ROLLING CONSTRAINT

The differential-drive model is built around the rolling constraint: under ideal conditions the wheels roll without lateral slip at the contact point. This constraint links wheel motion to the robot body's motion.

03 · DISCRETE DEAD-RECKONED ODOMETRY

Integrate tiny motions
into a trajectory.

A discrete odometry node repeatedly estimates how far each wheel travelled during a sampling interval, converts that into body motion, and updates the pose.

odometry_update()
ΔsL = r · ΔθL
ΔsR = r · ΔθR

Δs = (ΔsR + ΔsL) / 2
Δθ = (ΔsR - ΔsL) / L

x ← x + Δs · cos(θ + Δθ/2)
y ← y + Δs · sin(θ + Δθ/2)
θ ← θ + Δθ

repeat at each update → estimated pose
WHY THE MID-HEADING TERM?

Don't blindly integrate in the old direction.

During a curved motion the robot's heading changes over the interval. A midpoint-style heading θ + Δθ/2 gives a useful discrete approximation for the direction associated with the motion increment.

Sign conventions matter. Pick a consistent positive direction for wheel rotation and heading. A sign error can make a mathematically neat implementation produce a physically wrong trajectory.
Implementation challenge: your node must maintain the previous wheel state, compute increments, update pose and publish the result. The source specification does not prescribe a particular programming language or exact topic/frame names for this node, so use the laboratory's established ROS 2 conventions.

06 · WHERE ODOMETRY GOES WRONG

The equation can be right
and the trajectory can still be wrong.

The experiment explicitly asks you to attribute observed error to wheel-radius error, track-width error, wheel slip and encoder resolution. These do not all behave the same way.

r

Wheel-radius error

If the actual effective radius differs from the value used by your model, wheel travel is systematically scaled. Distance estimates can drift even when the robot behaves consistently.

L

Track-width error

If the assumed distance between wheel contact lines is wrong, the inferred angular motion is biased. This is especially visible in turning and closed-loop trajectories.

🛞

Wheel slip

The rolling model assumes ideal contact. Slip makes encoder-derived travel disagree with actual ground motion and can vary with surface, acceleration and direction.

#

Encoder resolution

Finite encoder resolution means motion is represented in discrete increments. This contributes measurement quantisation and can matter differently at different speeds and distances.

↻

Systematic vs random

A repeatable bias can accumulate predictably. Random slip or disturbances may vary between trials. The experiment asks you to use repeated trials to separate these behaviours.

⚙️

Model vs reality

Odometry is not “wrong” simply because it disagrees with physical measurement. The disagreement is evidence about the model, parameters and measurement assumptions.

08 · THE EXPERIMENT THAT SEPARATES MODEL ERROR FROM SLIP

Same square.
Different surface + direction.

The specification asks for repeated trials on more than one surface and in both rotational directions. This is not busywork: changing surface and turning direction changes the evidence available about systematic error and wheel slip.

↻ Clockwise circuit

Record closure error and compare the measured and estimated trajectory. Note which side/turns show the largest discrepancy.

↺ Counter-clockwise circuit

Repeat the same style of trial in the opposite rotational direction. A change in error pattern can reveal direction-dependent effects such as asymmetric slip or parameter bias.

🧱

Surface A

Use the first specified laboratory surface. Keep the trajectory procedure consistent and record the closure error.

🪵

Surface B

Repeat on a second surface as specified by the laboratory. Compare whether the error remains consistent or changes substantially.

Interpretation challenge: if the error changes strongly with surface or direction, be cautious about blaming only wheel radius or track width. The experiment is designed to make you investigate wheel slip and systematic effects using repeated evidence.
09 · ERROR METRICS

Turn “it missed the start”
into numbers.

The post-lab asks you to report closure error normalised per metre travelled and per radian turned, with mean and standard deviation over trials.

📏

Closure error

e = distance(Pend, Pstart)

Use the physical/defined position comparison required by your laboratory procedure.

📐

Per metre travelled

em = e / D

Normalises closure error by total travelled distance D.

↻

Per radian turned

erad = e / |Θ|

Normalises error by total absolute heading change |Θ| over the trial.

📊

Trial statistics

mean ± standard deviation

Summarise repeated trials to distinguish typical behaviour from trial-to-trial variation.

Important: use the exact error definition and measurement convention specified by your instructor/observation sheet when reporting final results. The source specification gives the normalisation requirements but not a complete prescribed statistical formula.
