00 · THE MISSION

Welcome to the
sensor showdown.

Experiment 08 builds directly on the sensing work from the earlier experiments. But this time the question is comparative: under what conditions does each ranging sensor perform well, degrade, or become unavailable?

🧪

You design the test matrix

The experiment gives the factors — distance, object size, surface finish, incidence angle, illumination and scan-plane/height relationship. Your group must justify the levels chosen.

📏

You need known references

Objects of known size, material and orientation are placed at tape-measured reference distances so measured performance can be compared with a reference.

📊

You quantify performance

Compute and compare bias, standard deviation, RMSE, relative error and availability from measured data.

🧠

You explain failures

A good engineer does not stop at “sensor failed”. You connect each failure to the underlying sensing physics and then decide whether the sensor is suitable for the application.

The mindset shift: there is no universal “best sensor”. A sensor is good or bad for a condition and application. Your evidence must justify the selection.
01 · KNOW YOUR COMPETITORS

LiDAR vs RGB-D.
Same question. Different physics.

The pre-lab asks you to review the physical principles of laser time-of-flight ranging and stereo depth estimation. Keep that physics in mind throughout the experiment: it is the bridge between the measurement and the explanation.

SENSOR A

📡 LiDAR

Laser-based ranging
  • Produces range measurements from laser returns.
  • In the experiment, evaluate its behaviour as object distance and geometry change.
  • Pay attention to surface finish, incidence angle and the relationship between the target and the scan plane.
  • Use measured bias, dispersion and availability rather than intuition to judge performance.
SENSOR B

📷 RGB-D

RGB image + depth estimate
  • Produces depth information associated with image observations.
  • Evaluate its response to object distance, object size, surface finish and illumination.
  • Pay attention to depth availability and error/bias under changed visual conditions.
  • Use the same reference objects and distances wherever the test matrix makes comparison meaningful.

Do not throw away “bad” samples automatically. A failure or invalid return is part of sensor performance. Record it, quantify availability, and explain why it happened.
04 · PERFORMANCE METRICS

Turn raw measurements
into evidence.

The learning outcome explicitly requires bias, standard deviation, RMSE, relative error and availability. These answer different questions and should not be collapsed into one score.

μ error

Bias

Shows the average signed tendency of the measurement to be above or below the reference.

σ

Standard deviation

Describes spread/dispersion across repeated observations.

RMSE

Root mean square error

Combines the magnitude of errors and is sensitive to larger deviations.

%

Availability

Describes how often a usable measurement is actually available under the tested condition.

Bias

bias = mean(e)

RMSE

RMSE = √mean(e²)

Availability

usable / total × 100%

06 · EXPLAIN THE FAILURE

Don't say “it failed.”
Say why.

One of the strongest parts of this experiment is the requirement to explain each observed failure in terms of underlying sensing physics. A failure is evidence when you can connect it to a condition.

🧱 Surface finish

Change the material/surface finish and observe whether the error or availability changes.

Question: what changed at the sensor-target interaction?

📐 Incidence angle

Rotate the target and see whether the measured range or availability changes.

Question: how does target orientation affect the sensed return/observation?

💡 Illumination

Compare the specified lighting conditions, especially when evaluating RGB-D depth.

Question: did visual sensing conditions change measurement availability or error?

📦 Object size

Use known target sizes and determine whether both sensors consistently observe the target.

Question: is the sensed region actually large enough for reliable observation?

📡 Scan-plane height

Move the target relative to the LiDAR scan plane and document missed or partial observations.

Question: is the sensing geometry intersecting the object?

📏 Distance

Perform the distance sweep and examine whether error and availability remain stable.

Question: does performance depend on range?
Physics-first rule: “The value was wrong” is an observation. “The value was wrong because condition X changed the sensing geometry/observation process” is an engineering explanation.
07 · FAILURE IS DATA

Availability matters
as much as accuracy.

❌ Invalid / missing return

The sensor does not provide a usable range for a condition.

Record it. Do not silently remove it.

📉 High dispersion

Measurements are available but vary substantially across repeated samples.

Standard deviation exposes this behaviour.

↗️ Systematic bias

Measurements consistently sit above or below the reference.

Mean signed error exposes directional bias.

💥 Large outliers

Occasional measurements are far from the reference.

RMSE is sensitive to large deviations.

📐 Geometry-dependent failure

Performance changes strongly with target angle or height.

Use the physical geometry to explain the pattern.

💡 Condition-dependent failure

Performance changes with illumination or surface condition.

Compare conditions instead of averaging them together.

09 · SENSOR SELECTION

Stop asking “which is better?”
Ask “better for what?”

The engineering deliverable is a one-page sensor selection guide covering emergency stop, pallet-edge detection, low-obstacle detection, glass-walled corridors and warehouse mapping. Your selection must be based on measured evidence.

🛑 Emergency stop

Prioritise dependable detection and appropriate availability in the actual operating geometry.

Use your measured evidence → defend the choice.

📦 Pallet-edge detection

Consider target geometry, object size, scan-plane relationship and observed availability.

Ask: does the sensor reliably see the edge?

⬇️ Low-obstacle detection

Pay special attention to sensing-plane geometry and whether the target lies within the observable region.

Geometry can dominate the decision.

🪟 Glass-walled corridors

Use the surface/material results and observed failure conditions rather than assuming both sensors behave alike.

Explain the physics behind the evidence.

🏭 Warehouse mapping

Consider range performance, availability, repeatability and the environmental conditions of the application.

Select based on the measured matrix.

🧠 Hybrid thinking

The experiment can reveal complementary strengths. The correct engineering recommendation need not be “one sensor always wins”.

Evidence can justify conditional selection.
Defend, don't declare. Your selection guide should point from application requirement → measured condition → metric → observed failure/strength → sensor choice.
10 · HOW TO WRITE THE COMPARISON

Build an argument
from the matrix.

Step 1 · State the condition

“At this reference distance, target size, surface, angle, illumination and scan-plane condition…”

Step 2 · Show the metric

Report bias, standard deviation, RMSE, relative error and availability as appropriate.

Step 3 · Compare sensors

State which sensor performed better under that specific condition and by what evidence.

Step 4 · Explain physically

Connect the observed difference or failure to the relevant sensing principle and geometry.

Strong sentence: “Under condition X, sensor A showed lower RMSE and higher availability than sensor B; the difference is consistent with the observed sensing-physics behaviour under that condition.” Then show the numbers.
