00 · THE MISSION

Welcome to the
capstone.

Experiment 10 is deliberately different from the earlier guided experiments. You are given what the robot must accomplish and the safety envelope. The design itself is yours to create and defend.

🎯

The requirement

Move the robot from the specified start pose to a goal pose six metres away through an obstacle field without contact.

🚫

What is NOT supplied

No navigation architecture, sensor arbitration policy, threshold set, control law, state machine or recovery behaviour is supplied.

🧠

Your job

Decide and defend the architecture, implement the navigator, parameterise it, test it, induce failures and explain the observed behaviour.

🛡️

The non-negotiable

The safety limiter must remain independent from the goal-seeking policy. The robot's desire to reach the goal can never override the safety envelope.

The mindset shift: Experiments 4–9 gave you measurements. Experiment 10 asks: what engineering decisions should those measurements cause you to make?
01 · ARCHITECTURE

Build the navigator
as layers.

A robust local navigator should not be one giant “go-to-goal” function. Separate the responsibility for deciding where to go, avoiding obstacles, handling abnormal states and enforcing safety.

02 · STATE MACHINE

Don't code a mess.
Design a state machine.

The laboratory explicitly asks for a state machine. The state machine is your answer to the question: “Given what is happening right now, what should the robot do next?”

01

🎯 SEEK

When the path is safe, move toward the goal.

  • Compute goal direction
  • Generate desired motion
  • Respect velocity limits
02

🧱 AVOID

When obstacle proximity becomes unsafe, abandon or modify the direct goal-seeking command.

  • Choose free space
  • Reduce speed
  • Turn safely
03

🔄 RECOVER

When progress stalls or the policy enters an undesirable configuration, execute a defined recovery strategy.

  • Stop / reorient
  • Reassess sensors
  • Resume only when safe
04

⚠️ DEGRADED

When an expected sensor becomes unavailable, switch to the explicitly designed safe response.

  • Detect dropout
  • Limit motion
  • Recover or stop
State-machine question: for every transition, write the condition. “Obstacle detected” is not enough — define what threshold, persistence, hysteresis or health condition causes the transition, and justify it using evidence from Experiments 4–9.
03 · PARAMETER TABLE

Your parameters need
a reason.

The strongest requirement in this experiment is that thresholds and gains must be justified against your own measurements from Experiments 4 to 9. Do not invent a number and call it “tuned”.

🛑 Stop distance

Distance at which the safety behaviour must prevent continued unsafe motion.

SOURCE → Exp 05 stopping-distance evidence

🐢 Slow-down distance

Distance at which the navigator begins reducing speed before the hard safety boundary.

SOURCE → Exp 05 reactive-control data

📈 Steering gain

Gain used by the goal-seeking or avoidance controller to convert directional error into angular velocity.

SOURCE → measured robot response / prior trials

🚗 Velocity limit

Maximum command allowed by the navigation policy before the independent safety limiter applies its own constraints.

SOURCE → platform behaviour + Exp 04/05 evidence

📡 Sensor health threshold

Criterion for deciding whether a sensor stream is sufficiently healthy to support navigation.

SOURCE → Exp 03 sensor communication/quality evidence

🧭 Pose tolerance

Goal-distance and heading criteria used to decide when the robot has reached the target.

SOURCE → localisation / odometry evidence

🔁 Recovery timeout

Maximum time or condition before the navigator changes state when progress is not occurring.

SOURCE → your design hypothesis + trials

↔️ Hysteresis

Prevents rapid switching between states when a measurement hovers around a threshold.

SOURCE → measured sensor variability

🧱 Clearance margin

Additional buffer between detected obstacle distance and the minimum allowed robot clearance.

SOURCE → Exp 05 + robot geometry
Engineering rule: every parameter row should answer three questions — What is it? Where did the value come from? Why is that value appropriate?
04 · DESIGN THE CONTROL LAW

Goal seeking should be
simple. Safety should be stronger.

🎯

Goal direction

Use the current robot pose and goal pose to determine the desired direction of travel. The exact formulation is your design decision.

🌀

Heading control

Convert heading error into a controlled angular command while respecting your experimentally justified gain and velocity limits.

📡

Obstacle influence

Modify or replace the goal command when obstacle geometry makes direct motion unsafe. Potential-field, switching or blended approaches are possible design choices.

🛡️

Independent safety layer

The final command is constrained by the safety limiter regardless of what the goal-seeking controller wants.

06 · THE SAFETY LIMITER

The navigator can be clever.
The limiter must be strict.

The experiment specifically requires an independent safety limiter. This is a safety architecture principle: the component deciding what the robot wants to do must not be the only component deciding what the robot is allowed to do.

🧱 Clearance constraint

Constrain forward motion based on measured obstacle distance and the experimentally determined safety envelope.

Evidence → Exp 05 stopping-distance measurements

⚡ Velocity constraint

Prevent candidate commands from exceeding the permitted velocity range.

Evidence → robot response + safety requirement

🛑 Emergency stop behaviour

Define what happens when the safety boundary is crossed or sensor health becomes unacceptable.

Evidence → Exp 05 + sensor reliability work

🔒 Independence

The safety limiter must be capable of overriding the goal-seeking policy.

Architecture → safety must dominate intent

⚠️ Sensor dropout

An unexpected sensor loss must produce a defined safe response rather than undefined behaviour.

Test → induced dropout during acceptance

🔄 Recovery

After a safety intervention, resume motion only when the recovery condition has actually been satisfied.

Design → explicit state transition
Golden rule: “Reach the goal” is a performance objective. “Do not contact an obstacle” is a safety constraint. If they conflict, safety wins.

09 · ACCEPTANCE TEST

Don't demo it once.
Try to break it.

The acceptance test is deliberately broader than “the robot reached the goal”. The post-lab requires results over three trials per scenario, including success, time to goal, path length, minimum clearance and any contact.

01

Normal obstacle field

Run the specified scenario repeatedly. Measure whether the robot reaches the goal without contact and how efficiently it does so.

02

Constrained / challenging layout

Use the defined acceptance scenarios to expose the weaknesses of the navigation policy and recovery logic.

03

Sensor dropout

Introduce the unannounced sensor dropout and observe whether the safety limiter and state handling behave as designed.

⏱ TIME TO GOAL

Three trials matter. One successful run proves that the robot succeeded once. Repeated trials give evidence about reliability and variability.
10 · SENSOR DROPOUT

The moment your design
gets real.

The brief includes an unannounced sensor-dropout test. This is not a surprise for the sake of drama; it checks whether sensor health, state handling and the safety limiter were actually designed rather than assumed.

1️⃣

Detect

Define what evidence indicates that the expected sensor is unavailable or no longer trustworthy.

2️⃣

Contain

Prevent the robot from continuing with an unsafe command based on stale or missing information.

3️⃣

Transition

Move to a defined degraded, recovery or safe-stop state.

4️⃣

Recover

Resume only when your explicitly defined recovery conditions have been satisfied.

Defence question: “What exactly would your software do if the LiDAR/RGB-D stream disappeared right now?” If the answer is “it depends”, your state machine is incomplete.

Before implementation,
defend the blueprint.

🗺️

Architecture diagram

Show sensors, state estimation, navigation policy, safety limiter, commands and the information flow between them.

🔀

State machine

Show states, transitions, triggers, safety conditions and recovery paths.

📋

Parameter table

List every important threshold/gain, its value, source of evidence and reason for selection.

🧪

Acceptance plan

Define how you will measure success, time, path length, minimum clearance, contact and failure modes.

Design review rule: implementation is not permitted until the architecture, state machine and parameter table are sufficiently defined for someone else to understand your design.
12 · FAILURE MODES

Every failure should
teach you something.

🔁 Oscillation

The navigator repeatedly switches steering direction or states instead of making progress.

Investigate → thresholds, gains, hysteresis, policy interaction

🧱 Corner trap

The local policy finds itself in a geometry from which the selected obstacle-avoidance strategy cannot escape.

Investigate → potential-field / switching limitations

🐢 Excessive caution

The robot remains safe but takes an impractically long time or path to reach the goal.

Investigate → clearance margin, speed, policy tuning

🏎️ Over-aggressive motion

The robot reaches the goal quickly but loses clearance or approaches obstacles too aggressively.

Investigate → velocity limits and safety thresholds

⚠️ Dropout blindness

The navigator continues behaving as if a missing sensor were still healthy.

Investigate → sensor health and state transition logic

🎯 Goal fixation

The goal-seeking policy dominates obstacle information and produces unsafe candidate commands.

Investigate → arbitration and independent limiter

14 · POST-LAB CHECKLIST

Before your oral defence,
have the evidence.

✓Architecture: design diagram showing sensing, estimation, policy, safety and command flow.
✓State machine: states, transitions, triggers and recovery behaviour documented.
✓Parameter table: every important threshold/gain has a value and evidence-based justification.
✓ROS 2 package: navigator integrated and parameterised with a reproducible launch configuration.
✓Acceptance results: three trials per scenario with success, time, path length, minimum clearance and contact recorded.
✓Dropout: behaviour of the independent safety limiter documented.
✓Revision: design changes made during implementation recorded with reasons.
✓Defence: explain what worked, what failed, why it failed and what measurement/design decision would improve it.
Best defence answer: “We chose this architecture because… We selected this threshold from… The measurement showed… When the sensor dropped out, the state changed from… to… The limiter then… Our failure mode was… and we changed… because…”
15 · MISSION COMPLETE

You were not given
a navigator.
You designed one.

Experiment 10 is the point where the laboratory stops asking you to implement a prescribed solution. You must transform evidence from Experiments 4–9 into an engineered local navigation system — one that seeks the goal, handles obstacles, survives degraded sensing, recovers from failure and remains bounded by an independent safety limiter.

The final question is not “Did the robot move?” It is: “Can you defend why your robot moved the way it did — and prove that it remained safe?”
